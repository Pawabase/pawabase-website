A **backend as a service (BaaS)** is a ready-made backend you rent instead of build. It gives your app a database with an API, user
sign-in, access rules, file storage, realtime updates, background work and email, all run for you, and you use it from your web app,
mobile app or server through an SDK or plain HTTP. You write the product. The provider runs the machinery underneath it.

That's the one-sentence answer. The rest of this guide is the long one: what a backend actually does, what a BaaS replaces, how a
single request travels through one, where the idea came from, what it's good and bad at, the worries people raise (lock-in, cost,
scale, security) and how to choose one without regretting it two years later.

<Callout type="tip" title="The short version">
A BaaS turns the backend from something you build and operate into something you call. You trade some control over the server for a
great deal of time: no auth system to write, no database to patch, no file server to secure, no job queue to babysit. It's the right
choice for most new products, and the wrong one only when your product's core *is* unusual backend infrastructure.
</Callout>

## First, what is a backend?

Every app has two halves. The **frontend** is what people see and touch: the screens of a mobile app, the pages of a web app. The
**backend** is everything that has to happen somewhere other than the person's device, because it must be shared, trusted or
remembered.

Think of a food delivery app. The menu you scroll is the frontend. But the menu itself lives somewhere. When you place an order,
something has to check you're signed in, confirm the restaurant is open, save the order, charge your card, tell the restaurant, send
you a receipt, and push "your rider is two minutes away" to your phone. None of that can run only on your phone: other people need
to see it, it must not be tampered with, and it has to still be there tomorrow. That's the backend.

In practice almost every backend does the same handful of jobs:

- **Store data** (users, orders, messages, products) in a database, and keep it consistent when many people write at once.
- **Expose that data through an API**, so apps can read and change it over the internet.
- **Know who is calling**: sign-up, sign-in, passwords, social logins, sessions, two-factor codes, password resets.
- **Decide what each caller may do**: you may read your own orders, not someone else's; an admin may refund, a customer may not.
- **Keep files**: profile pictures, receipts, uploads, with links that only the right people can open.
- **Push changes live**, so a chat message or an order status appears without refreshing.
- **Do work in the background**: send emails, resize images, retry a failed payment, run a nightly report.
- **Talk to other systems**: receive webhooks from a payment provider, send events to a CRM, call AI models.
- **Stay up and stay safe**: backups, monitoring, scaling under load, security patches, logs when something breaks.

Building that list from scratch is a lot of work, and most of it is the same work in every product. That repetition is exactly what
a BaaS exists to remove.

## So what is a backend as a service?

A backend as a service packages those common jobs into one hosted product. Instead of writing a sign-in system, you switch sign-in
on and call it. Instead of designing an API for your orders table, the BaaS gives you one the moment the table exists. Instead of
setting up a file server and signing download links, you upload to a bucket and get a link back.

![Your apps on top (web, mobile, an AI agent, your own server) call the backend as a service in the middle: database and API, sign-in and users, access rules, file storage, realtime, logic and jobs, email, webhooks and events. Underneath, servers, scaling, backups, patches and monitoring are run for you.](./assets/baas-stack.png "Your apps call the building blocks. The provider runs everything underneath them.")

Three things make something a BaaS rather than just "hosting":

1. **You consume building blocks, not machines.** You never pick a server size to run your sign-in system. You call sign-in.
2. **Your apps talk to it directly.** A web or mobile app can call a BaaS from the browser or phone, safely, because the access rules
   run on the BaaS, not in the app. A traditional backend needs your own server in the middle for that.
3. **Someone else operates it.** Uptime, scaling, backups and security patches are the provider's job.

You still write code. You write the parts that make your product *yours*: the screens, the business rules, the workflows. What you
stop writing is the plumbing that every product needs and no customer ever thanks you for.

## Where the idea came from

The term took off around 2011, when mobile apps exploded and small teams found themselves needing a backend before they had anyone to
build one. Early services such as Parse and Firebase offered a database, user accounts and push notifications you could call
straight from an iPhone or Android app. Parse was bought by Facebook in 2013 and shut down in 2017, which taught the industry an
important lesson about lock-in that we'll come back to. Firebase was bought by Google in 2014 and grew into one of the best-known
platforms in the category.

The next wave, roughly from 2020, rebuilt the idea around open technology: real SQL databases (very often Postgres) instead of
proprietary ones, open-source code you could run yourself, and standard APIs. That answered the lock-in worry and made BaaS a
serious option for products expected to grow into businesses, not just for prototypes.

The wave happening now is about **completeness**. Early platforms gave you a database and auth and left the rest (workflows, email,
background jobs, organizations for business customers, AI) to other services you glued together. Newer platforms try to carry the
whole backend: the things a product needs after launch, not just on day one.

## What's inside a BaaS, block by block

Not every platform has every block, and the depth of each varies enormously. That variation is most of what separates them. Here is
what each block does and the questions worth asking about it.

### Database and data API

The heart of every BaaS. You define tables (or collections) and the platform gives you an API to create, read, update and delete
records, usually with filtering, sorting, pagination and relations ("this order, with its items and its customer").

What to look at: is it a real database you can query with SQL and export with standard tools, or a proprietary store? Can you change
the shape of a table safely once it holds data, with a history of those changes? Can you add indexes when lists get slow?

### Sign-in and users

Accounts, passwords, email verification, password resets, social logins (Google, GitHub, Apple…), magic links, passkeys,
multi-factor codes, sessions and devices. This is the block teams most underestimate: a basic sign-in form takes a day, a *secure*
one with resets, rate limits, lockouts and session revocation takes weeks.

If you sell to companies, look further: **organizations** (a customer company with many members), roles inside them, invitations,
verified company domains, and enterprise single sign-on (SSO), because business customers ask for these early.

### Access rules

The block that decides whether a BaaS is safe to call from a browser. Access rules say, for every operation, who may do it: "a
signed-in user may read orders where `user_id` is their own id", "only an admin of the organization may delete a project".

What to look at: can one rule protect everything (records, files, realtime channels and custom logic), or does each have its own
rule system? Most security incidents in young products are not clever attacks. They're two rules that disagree, or one that was
forgotten.

### File storage

Buckets for uploads with size and type limits, public or private files, links that expire, and often image resizing on the fly.
Good storage applies the same access rules as your data, so "only the owner may download their invoice" is one rule, not a second
system.

### Realtime

Live updates pushed to connected apps: chat messages, notifications, a dashboard that ticks, "Ada is typing", who's online. Some
platforms stream database changes; others give you channels you publish to. Look at how realtime respects your access rules, since a
live feed that leaks other people's data is still a leak.

### Logic: functions and workflows

Not everything is "save this record". Placing an order might check stock, charge a card, notify the restaurant and email a receipt.
BaaS platforms handle this in one of two ways:

- **Functions**: code you write and deploy, which the platform runs when called or when something happens.
- **Workflows (flows)**: steps you connect visually or declare, such as "when an order is created, charge the card, then send this
  email", with retries, logs and history built in.

Many platforms offer one or both. What matters is whether your logic can use the same data, users and rules as everything else,
without you wiring credentials between services.

### Background jobs, schedules and events

Work that shouldn't make a user wait: sending a thousand emails, processing an upload, retrying a payment that failed, a report
every night at 3 a.m. Look for queues with retries, schedules (cron), and events that other parts of the system can react to.

### Email

Verification emails, password resets, receipts, notifications. Some platforms only send their own auth emails and leave everything
else to a separate email service. More complete ones let you design templates and send any message, with delivery logs.

### Webhooks and integrations

Your backend rarely lives alone. Payment providers, messaging services and CRMs send you webhooks ("this payment succeeded"), and you
send events out to them. A BaaS that verifies incoming webhooks and retries outgoing ones saves a surprising amount of fragile code.

### AI

The newest block. Calling AI models from an app has the same problem as any secret: you can't put a provider's API key in a browser.
Platforms now offer gateways that keep keys on the server and apply your access rules to who may call which model, plus agents,
search by meaning (vectors), and connections for AI coding assistants through the Model Context Protocol (MCP).

### Operations: the parts you notice when things go wrong

A dashboard to browse data and users, request logs, error tracking, usage against your limits, backups you can restore, and
separate environments (development, staging, production) so you never test on real customers. These don't show up in a demo, and
they decide how your 2 a.m. incidents go.

## How a request travels through a BaaS

It helps to follow one request end to end. Say a customer taps **Place order** in your mobile app.

<Steps>

### The app calls the API

The app sends the new order to the BaaS's data API, with the customer's sign-in token attached. No server of yours sits in between.

```http title="What the app sends"
POST /rest/v1/orders
Authorization: Bearer <the customer's sign-in token>
Content-Type: application/json

{ "restaurant_id": "r_19", "items": [{ "dish_id": "d_4", "quantity": 2 }] }
```

### The BaaS checks who is calling

It verifies the token: who this is, whether the session is still valid, whether they belong to an organization.

### The access rules decide

The rule for creating orders runs: is this a signed-in customer, and are they creating an order for themselves? If not, the request
stops here with a clear refusal, whatever the app tried to send.

### The data is validated and saved

Fields are checked against the table's definition (`quantity` must be a positive number) and the order is written to the database.

### Events fire

Saving the order emits an event, `orders.created`. Anything listening can react, without slowing down the customer's request.

### Background work runs

A workflow subscribed to that event charges the card, notifies the restaurant over a webhook, and sends a receipt email, each step
retried if it fails and logged either way.

### Everyone sees it live

The restaurant's dashboard, connected over realtime, shows the new order the moment it's saved.

</Steps>

In a traditional setup, every one of those steps is code you'd write, deploy and keep running, across several services. With a BaaS,
most of them are configuration, and the code you do write is about your restaurant business, not about tokens and retries.

## BaaS compared with the other ways to build a backend

A BaaS isn't the only way to avoid building everything from scratch. Here's how it compares with the common alternatives.

| Approach | You still build and run | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Your own backend** | Everything | Unusual infrastructure |
| **PaaS** | The whole backend app | Writing your own backend |
| **Serverless functions** | Data, auth, rules, wiring | Glue and small APIs |
| **Headless CMS** | Users, logic, transactions | Sites and catalogues |
| **Low-code tools** | A customer-facing backend | Internal operations |
| **BaaS** | Your product logic and screens | Most new products |

"Your own backend" means a framework such as Django, Rails, Laravel or Express on cloud servers: total control, and every item on
the list at the top of this guide to build and keep running. A PaaS (platform as a service) runs that code for you but leaves the
backend itself to you. Serverless functions remove the servers, not the work of building auth, data and rules.

These mix well. Plenty of teams use a BaaS for data, auth and storage, and still keep a small server of their own for one unusual
job. A good BaaS makes that easy rather than all-or-nothing.

## When a BaaS is the right choice

- **You're building a product, not infrastructure.** Your edge is the experience, the workflow, the market, not a novel database.
- **You're a small team.** Two engineers can't build auth, storage, realtime, jobs and email *and* the product, and keep it all safe.
- **Speed matters.** Going from idea to something real customers use in weeks instead of months is often the whole game.
- **You want security you didn't have to invent.** Password storage, token handling and session revocation done by people who do
  nothing else.
- **You have web, mobile and AI clients.** One backend all of them call directly, with the same rules, beats three API layers.

## When it isn't

- **Your core product is unusual backend infrastructure**: a new database, a trading engine with microsecond needs, a video
  transcoding pipeline. You'll want to own that layer.
- **You have strict rules about where data lives and who operates it**, and no provider meets them. Many platforms can be
  self-hosted or run in specific regions, so check before ruling the category out.
- **You need a feature the platform doesn't have and can't host next to it.** That's why the escape hatches below matter.

## The honest worries, answered

### "We'll be locked in."

The fear is real. Parse's shutdown in 2017 forced thousands of apps to migrate in a hurry. The defence is to pick a platform whose
foundations are standard: a real SQL database (Postgres is the common choice) you can connect to and export with ordinary tools,
standard sign-in protocols (OAuth, OpenID Connect, SAML), files you can download in bulk, and ideally an open-source or
self-hostable option. Then leaving is a project, not a rewrite.

### "It won't scale."

Modern platforms run on the same databases and clouds you'd use yourself, and serve products with millions of users. The scaling
questions to ask are concrete: can I get a dedicated database when shared isn't enough, can I add indexes and read replicas, what
are the rate limits, and what happens when I hit a plan limit: does the app slow down or stop?

### "It'll get expensive."

At small scale a BaaS is almost always cheaper than the engineering time it replaces. At large scale it depends on the pricing
model, so model your own numbers: requests, database size, storage, bandwidth, emails, and what happens to the bill if you double.
Watch for prices in a currency you don't earn in, and for limits that force a big plan jump for one small need.

### "Is it secure to call from the browser?"

Yes, when access rules are done properly, and that "when" is the whole point. The keys in your app are public by design. Security
comes from rules evaluated on the server for every request. Test your rules like you test your code, and prefer platforms where one
rule system covers data, files, realtime and logic, so there's no forgotten corner.

### "What about compliance?"

Ask where data is stored and processed, whether you can choose the region, what certifications the provider holds, whether you can
export and delete a person's data on request, and whether there's a data processing agreement. These answers matter more every year,
and especially if your customers are businesses or you operate under data protection laws.

## How to choose a BaaS: a checklist

Work through these with your own product in mind, not a demo app:

1. **The data model.** Is it a real SQL database? Can you query it, export it and change table shapes safely with history?
2. **Access rules.** Can one rule protect records, files, channels and logic? Can you test rules before shipping them?
3. **Sign-in depth.** Social logins, passkeys, multi-factor, organizations and roles, invitations, enterprise SSO if you sell to
   companies.
4. **Logic.** Functions, workflows or both? Do they share the same data and rules, with retries and history?
5. **Background work.** Queues, retries, schedules, events and outgoing webhooks you can inspect.
6. **Email.** Templates and delivery for any message, not just auth emails.
7. **AI.** A way to call models without exposing keys, rules on who may call them, and support for AI coding assistants.
8. **Operations.** Separate environments, backups you have actually restored, request logs, usage dashboards, a status page.
9. **The exit.** Standard database, standard protocols, bulk export, a self-hosting option.
10. **Pricing.** Your numbers at today's size, at ten times, and what happens at each limit.

## Where BaaS is heading

Three shifts are under way. First, **completeness**: platforms are absorbing the jobs teams used to glue on from other vendors
(workflows, email, background jobs, organizations, observability) because every extra vendor is another bill, another set of users
and another set of rules that can disagree. Second, **AI**: both as a feature (model gateways, agents, search by meaning) and as a
user, with AI coding assistants building and changing backends through protocols like MCP. Third, **openness**: real SQL databases,
standard protocols and self-hosting are becoming the expectation rather than a differentiator.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does BaaS stand for?

Backend as a service. It's sometimes written MBaaS (mobile backend as a service), from the era when the category served mobile apps
first.

### Is a BaaS the same as serverless?

They overlap but aren't the same. "Serverless" usually means you run your own code without managing servers, typically as functions.
A BaaS gives you ready-made backend building blocks (database, auth, storage…) so there's less code to write in the first place. Many
BaaS platforms include serverless functions as one of their blocks.

### Is Firebase a BaaS?

Yes. Firebase is one of the best-known examples, and it helped define the category in the 2010s.

### Do I still need a backend developer with a BaaS?

You need someone who thinks carefully about data models, access rules and workflows, because those decide whether your product is
correct and safe. What you no longer need is a team building and operating the plumbing underneath.

### Can I use a BaaS with my existing server or framework?

Usually, yes. Your own server can call a BaaS over HTTP like any client, typically with a secret server key. Many teams keep their
existing framework for one specialised job and use a BaaS for the rest.

### Can I move off a BaaS later?

It depends on the platform. If your data lives in a standard database you can export, sign-in uses standard protocols, and files can
be downloaded in bulk, moving is a planned project. If the data store is proprietary, it's much harder, so check before you start.

### Is a BaaS only for prototypes?

Not any more. Early platforms were mostly used for prototypes and small apps. Today's platforms run production products with large
user bases, especially those built on standard databases with dedicated database options for when shared resources aren't enough.
